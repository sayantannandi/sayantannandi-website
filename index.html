Being dependable can
become doing everything.

Some habits earn you trust early in your career. As your responsibility grows, you need to examine what those habits now cost your team.

You say yes before discussing capacity.

Perhaps you learned that a good student follows instructions and a good employee finds a way. At work, that can become accepting another deadline without naming what will slip.

A senior leader makes that choice visible: “We can bring this forward if we move the reporting upgrade to next week. My recommendation is to protect the customer deadline.”

You explain the work in technical detail.

The person allocating a global budget may never see your team at work. A list of releases or resolved tickets leaves them to work out why those things matter.

Explain who benefits, what changed and what evidence supports it. Your technical knowledge becomes more useful when another person can use it to make a decision.

You stay involved in every decision.

Being the person with every answer can make you the point where work queues up. A larger portfolio requires other people to make sound decisions without waiting for you.

Define ownership, decision boundaries and review points. For AI-assisted work, define what you can delegate and how you will check the result.

You treat your local target as the whole problem.

A decision that improves one team’s numbers may create cost or risk elsewhere. Global responsibility asks you to understand those consequences across customers, functions and regions.

You can start practicing that judgment in your current role, before a title or location changes.
