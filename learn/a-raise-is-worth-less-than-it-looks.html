A raise is worth less than it looks

I was promoted most cycles. My income nearly doubled in three years. My runway was eight months.

I rarely had to ask for more pay. For most of my career I was promoted most cycles, and each promotion came with a raise. It felt like moving forward. On paper, it was.

Then, at 33, after the best year of my career so far, I divided what we had by what our household cost each month. The answer was eight. Eight months of runway, after three years in which my income had nearly doubled.

Nobody had taken the money. We had spent it, a little at a time, in ways that each made sense. A bigger flat, because the old one felt small for the new title. A better car. Holidays that matched the people we now worked with. Every raise found a home within a few months.

That is why a raise is worth less than it looks. It is a number on your salary slip. What it buys you depends on where it goes next.

Take a made-up manager on ₹2,00,000 a month, with ₹16 lakh saved and costs of ₹2,00,000 a month. That is 8 months. She is promoted, and her pay rises by ₹40,000 a month. If her costs rise by ₹40,000 too, her runway falls to 6.7 months. She earns more and could last a shorter time.

If she moves ₹20,000 of the raise out on salary day and lets her costs rise by the other ₹20,000, a year later she has ₹18.4 lakh against costs of ₹2,20,000. That is 8.4 months, and climbing.

So the promotion is half the work. The other half is deciding, before the first new salary lands, how much of the raise is allowed to become a new cost. That decision does not need a meeting with anyone.

Your turn

Think of your last raise. How much of it is still yours today, and how much became a new cost?
