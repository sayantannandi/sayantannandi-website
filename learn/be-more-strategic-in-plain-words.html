Be more strategic, in plain words

The most common feedback good managers get, and the least explained. Three moves you can test this week.

The first time a boss told me to be more strategic, I nodded as if I understood. I did not. I walked out, decided it meant something about the bigger picture, and used the word "holistic" about sixteen times the next week. Nothing changed, except that I was now vague as well.

The feedback is fair. It is just hard to act on, because it sounds like an adjective instead of an instruction. Here is the instruction. "Be more strategic" means: I am paying you for the steps below, and I want to see evidence of the next one. That evidence comes in a few simple moves. Three matter most.

Say what you will not do. I once walked into a review with nine initiatives, color-coded, with a chart that took most of a weekend. The senior leader looked at it for a while and asked, "What did you take off this list?" Nothing. Then she said, "A plan that contains everything is just a wish list with dates." Cut to three. Under each thing you cut, write one line on what you accept will not happen.

Price it in money, not activity. "The platform has technical debt" is true, and nobody can act on it. "We lose about 25 engineer-weeks a quarter to rework, roughly ₹76 lakh a year" is the same fact in the currency the room thinks in.

Hand over a decision, not a situation. "We are three weeks behind, and working through it" is a weather report. "We will miss March. Three options. I would take the second, and I need a decision by Friday" is a recommendation the room can say yes or no to.

None of this needs a new title. It needs a different thing leaving your desk. The full set of moves is in chapter seven of the book.

Your turn

Take the plan you are working on now. What would you cut, and what are you accepting by cutting it?
