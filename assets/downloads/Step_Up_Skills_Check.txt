STEP UP SKILLS CHECK
Eight fictional situations. A reflection tool, not a leadership rating or promotion prediction.
Choose a response before reading the explanations at the end.

1. Explain business outcomes
Finance / A faster month-end close
A process trial reduced reconciliation time from 40 to 28 staff-hours in one close. Disputes also fell. Staffing and payroll have not changed, and the team has not checked whether the improvement will persist.
What is the most defensible update?
A. Report a 30% payroll saving and propose the same saving in every team.
B. Report 12 hours of released capacity in this close, with no payroll saving yet. Check time, volume and errors at the next close before a wider claim.
C. Wait a year before reporting anything, because one close cannot establish a permanent improvement.

2. Communicate a clear point
Procurement / A supplier decision
Your primary supplier has not confirmed a delivery needed next week. A backup supplier can cover the essential quantity at a known additional cost. Your director must approve that spend today to keep the option available.
How should you open the update?
A. Ask for approval today for the bounded backup order, explain the delivery risk and added cost, then supply the supporting detail.
B. Begin with a chronological account of every supplier conversation and finish by saying you are monitoring the situation.
C. Say the team is proactively aligning supplier resilience with strategic priorities, and offer more detail if requested.

3. Make a sound recommendation
Product / An uncertain new feature
Customer demand for a proposed feature is uncertain. A small prototype is feasible within existing authority and could test a specific demand assumption. A full build would use most of the team’s next quarter.
What is the strongest recommendation?
A. Commit the full quarter now because hesitation could mean losing the opportunity.
B. Present the prototype and full build as equally valid options, with no view on which to choose.
C. Recommend a bounded prototype, state the assumption it tests and the evidence that would support continuing, changing or stopping.

4. Set priorities and make room
Manufacturing / One specialist, two commitments
A planned maintenance task and an urgent customer change need the same specialist at the same time. You do not yet know which maintenance constraints are mandatory or who can approve a revised customer commitment.
What is the most useful next move?
A. Accept both dates and ask the specialist to find a way, keeping the resource conflict out of the leadership discussion.
B. Clarify the mandatory constraints and decision owners, then recommend a sequence or scope change that states what moves and whose agreement is needed.
C. Label both tasks top priority so each stakeholder can see that their request matters.

5. Build influence across teams
Sales and finance / A pricing exception
A large prospect wants a price exception. Finance is concerned about margin and setting a precedent. Sales believes the deal is valuable but has not yet explored the conditions under which finance could support it.
How would you start the conversation?
A. Understand finance’s margin and precedent constraints, then develop a bounded exception proposal with conditions and a review.
B. Ask a senior executive to overrule finance before discussing its concerns, because the prospect is important.
C. Repeat the potential deal value more forcefully until finance recognizes the opportunity.

6. Handle challenge and disagreement
Commercial review / Conflicting margin figures
In a review, a colleague challenges the margin figure supporting your recommendation. Their figure covers a different reporting period. You do not yet know whether comparing like-for-like would change the decision.
What is the strongest response?
A. Defend your original number immediately so the group does not lose confidence in your recommendation.
B. Accept the other figure without checking and withdraw the recommendation.
C. Acknowledge the difference, align the period and calculation basis, and explain how the corrected comparison affects the recommendation or next check.

7. Delegate with clear ownership
Service operations / A new shift lead
A capable shift lead is taking responsibility for scheduling within agreed staffing levels. Service and safety standards must hold. You want them to handle routine decisions without asking you for every change.
What is the clearest delegation?
A. Agree the service outcome, scheduling authority, safety and staffing limits, escalation triggers and a review point, then let the lead decide within those boundaries.
B. Tell the lead they own scheduling, while continuing to approve and revise every individual decision.
C. Ask the lead to prepare the schedule, while leaving all decisions and follow-up with you without discussing the distinction.

8. Bring the skills into one decision
Distribution / A delivery pilot
A delivery pilot could improve service but would affect warehouse capacity, sales promises and operating cost. You must recommend whether to proceed. Different teams own the constraints, and the evidence is incomplete.
What should your decision brief contain?
A. The operational details only, leaving each other team to infer the implications for its own work.
B. The outcome, evidence and uncertainty, credible options, recommended choice, trade-off, stakeholder constraints, decision authority, owner and review.
C. An inspiring vision for improved delivery, with details and decision rights to be agreed after launch.


EXPLANATIONS

1. Explain business outcomes
Strongest response for these facts: B
A: The time measure fell by 30%, but payroll did not change. The result does not yet support a cash-saving claim or the same effect in every team.
B: This keeps the measured benefit, distinguishes capacity from cash and names the evidence needed before making a broader claim.
C: The result is useful now if you describe its limits. You can report the observed benefit and propose a bounded follow-up without waiting for certainty.
Why it matters: At leadership level, a claim may become a budget or staffing decision. Separate what changed, what you infer and what still needs testing.
Practice: Take one outcome claim from an update. Separate the measurement, inference and next check.

2. Communicate a clear point
Strongest response for these facts: A
A: The decision, reason and cost are clear before the detail. Your director can understand what is needed and examine the basis for it.
B: The history may be useful evidence, but the director still needs to know which decision is required today. Monitoring alone does not preserve the backup option.
C: The language does not tell the director what to approve, by when or at what cost. A broad strategic phrase cannot replace a clear decision request.
Why it matters: As your audience’s responsibilities widen, your update should make the required decision understandable without making the evidence inaccessible.
Practice: Rewrite the opening of one update: decision, reason, consequence, then the supporting detail.

3. Make a sound recommendation
Strongest response for these facts: C
A: The size of the commitment is not supported by the current evidence. Urgency alone does not resolve uncertainty about demand.
B: The decision-maker needs your judgment about the evidence and commitment. Listing options without a reasoned preference leaves that work unfinished.
C: The recommendation matches the commitment to the available evidence. It turns uncertainty into a specific learning question and a later decision.
Why it matters: A recommendation is a choice supported by reasons. Its scope and reversibility should fit the uncertainty and the decision authority you have.
Practice: For one proposal, write what would change your recommendation and how you could obtain that evidence.

4. Set priorities and make room
Strongest response for these facts: B
A: Accepting both commitments does not create capacity. It transfers an unresolved decision to the specialist and hides the risk from those who can change it.
B: This establishes which choices are actually available, makes the capacity conflict explicit and takes the trade-off to the people with authority to agree it.
C: Equal labels do not resolve a shared-capacity conflict. A workable agreement needs a sequence, scope, resource or commitment change.
Why it matters: A senior priority decision must account for displaced work and real constraints. You cannot treat a mandatory requirement as optional or someone else’s approval as implied.
Practice: For a new priority, name what moves, the consequence and who must agree.

5. Build influence across teams
Strongest response for these facts: A
A: You are examining the responsibilities behind the objection. A bounded proposal can be assessed against both the opportunity and the constraints.
B: Escalation can be appropriate when needed, but this skips a useful attempt to understand and address the constraints. It may also leave the commercial risk unresolved.
C: Finance may already understand the revenue opportunity. Repetition does not answer the margin and precedent questions it is responsible for examining.
Why it matters: Influence grows when your proposal takes another function’s responsibilities seriously. Agreement should reflect a workable decision, not simply pressure.
Practice: Before one cross-team conversation, write the other team’s likely responsibility, risk and non-negotiable constraint.

6. Handle challenge and disagreement
Strongest response for these facts: C
A: Protecting the original statement before checking the comparison can make the recommendation less credible. Confidence should follow the evidence.
B: Being open to correction does not require accepting a non-comparable figure. First establish a consistent basis and assess whether the difference matters.
C: This keeps attention on the decision. You examine the disagreement, update if needed and make its consequence clear.
Why it matters: Handling challenge means remaining useful to the decision. Check the substance and be explicit about what has changed in your view.
Practice: Prepare a response that acknowledges a challenge, checks its basis and names the next decision.

7. Delegate with clear ownership
Strongest response for these facts: A
A: The lead has both responsibility and room to act. The boundaries and escalation triggers protect the outcome without requiring approval for every routine decision.
B: The stated ownership and actual authority conflict. Frequent intervention can keep the lead dependent on you even when you say they are responsible.
C: This delegates preparation, not ownership of the outcome. That can be a valid task, but it does not meet the stated goal of independent routine decisions.
Why it matters: Delegation needs an outcome and decision authority, with boundaries and review. Accountability remains with the leader, but routine judgment can move to the owner.
Practice: Rewrite one delegation using outcome, authority, limits, escalation and review.

8. Bring the skills into one decision
Strongest response for these facts: B
A: Operational detail is necessary but does not integrate the decision. The recommendation still needs the business outcome, shared constraints, trade-off and ownership.
B: This connects the reasoning to an actionable decision. It makes the consequences visible across teams and names how the result will be reviewed.
C: The vision does not settle whether or how to proceed. Launching before constraints and decision rights are clear can create commitments no one has agreed to own.
Why it matters: Senior work connects the parts. A clear brief should help others inspect the reasoning, agree the commitment and understand who can act next.
Practice: Use the One-Page Leadership Brief to bring one real decision together, then ask what remains unclear.
