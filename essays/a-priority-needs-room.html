Priorities / 4 min read

A new priority needs somewhere to go.

Updated 28 October 2026

Leadership means choosing what receives attention. Saying yes to everything leaves that decision unmade.

A new customer requirement arrives. The work matters, the deadline is close and the same specialist is already assigned to a maintenance task. The easy sentence is, “We will make both happen.” The difficult work starts after that sentence.

This fictional situation exposes a habit worth replacing as you grow into leadership: accepting a new commitment before deciding what must move. Making that choice is part of the broader contribution a senior role requires.

Priority is a decision about capacity.

A ranking does not create extra time. Before recommending a sequence, find out which commitments can move and which constraints are real. Some safety, legal or contractual requirements may not be yours to relax.

Then make the consequence explicit. If the customer change goes first, which work moves? If both dates remain, what additional capacity or reduced scope would make that possible?

Bring a recommendation, with the decision rights.

“The customer change and maintenance work need the same specialist. I recommend keeping the mandatory maintenance slot, reducing the initial customer change to the essential scope and scheduling the remaining work afterward. Operations and the account owner need to agree the revised customer commitment.”

The wording is only helpful if the underlying facts hold. If reduced scope would not meet the customer’s need, your recommendation needs to change.

Make the agreement inspectable.

Write down the chosen sequence, owner, changed commitment and review date. Check that the people affected heard the same decision. Quiet disagreement can return later as a missed handover or two conflicting promises.

Try it: complete this sentence for your newest priority: “If we do this now, we will move, stop or reduce ______, with agreement from ______.” If you cannot fill the blanks, you may still be discussing a wish rather than a workable priority.
