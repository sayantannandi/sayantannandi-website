“Show more ownership.”

Recognizable workplace situations.

What my feedback looks like

One weekly update,
read the way a panel reads it.

A made-up example, written to show the kind of feedback you get on every assignment.

What you sent
“This sprint the team completed the data lake migration for 3 more business units1 (9 of 14 done). We also onboarded 2 engineers and resolved 27 tickets2. Next sprint we will continue the remaining migrations.3”

How a panel reads it: a busy, reliable team. Nothing here needs a leader.

  1. What changed for those units? Faster month-end, cheaper reports, fewer manual fixes? Pick the one your VP cares about.
  2. Activity, not outcome. Cut it, or say what those tickets were costing.
  3. This is where you lead. What decision do you need, and from whom?
Your rewrite
“Finance and supply chain now get daily reports instead of weekly ones, from 9 of 14 business units. The last 5 depend on one vendor. I recommend we extend that contract by 3 months for ₹18 lakh, rather than rebuild in-house for ₹45 lakh over 6 months. I need your call by Friday.”

How a panel reads it now: someone who owns an outcome and makes a call.

What people ask before joining

Fair questions,
straight answers.

“I barely have time.”

About three hours a week, for six weeks. The assignments use work you are already doing: the update, the meeting, the decision.

“Why pay if I might leave anyway?”

Leaving is when these skills are tested hardest. Interviews for the next level look for exactly this. And the Live Classroom helps you decide whether to leave at all.

“I don’t want anyone at work to know.”

Use your personal email. I do not contact your employer or tag you publicly without your permission. Group sessions are recorded for members, so bring anonymized examples. The FAQ explains the group privacy boundaries.

“At my level, I shouldn’t need this.”

The higher you go, the less honest feedback you get. People stop telling you the truth just when it matters most.

“It’s politics. Training can’t fix that.”

Some of it is. A lot of it is being readable to people who can’t check your work. That part is a skill, and it can be practised.

How is this different from books and workshops?

You’ve read the books. Maybe your company sent you to a two-day leadership workshop. It made sense in the room. By Monday, the old habits were back.

These skills change when you practice on your own work, get honest feedback, and try again the next week.

Every Saturday you bring something real: an update, a decision, a disagreement. A small group works on it with you. And you keep showing up, because people expect you.
