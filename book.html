Earning Power × Exit Power = Optionality

It opens in a government house, with a boy doing the child's version of arithmetic: working out from the volume of his parents' voices how bad it was this time. Thirty years later, that boy had the best year of his career, a 40 percent raise, the title he had circled for three years, and then sat in a meeting where a decision was going wrong and said nothing, because a sum ran behind his face about what disagreeing would cost him.

That evening he opened a spreadsheet and ran a different sum. What he had, divided by what his life cost every month.

Income up steeply for three years. Runway flat. And the worst part: he had rebuilt his parents' condition at ten times the income. His father had security without money. He had money without security. Nobody in between had told him those were different things, or that the second one could produce the same silence in a meeting.

The book is what came out of that. Five parts. The formula and why it multiplies rather than adds. The first engine, what makes your time worth more once capability stops being scarce. The second engine, counted in months, and why commitments grow faster than income. Both engines applied to real decisions, including the pay cut that comes out differently for two identical men. And the loop: where it breaks, and what a second turn looks like. Four sums in the whole book, none of them hard, all of them meant to be run on your own life.

It is not a book about escaping work, retiring early, or being sensible with money. Optimise your earning power as hard as you can. Just do not let it be the only thing you optimise.

Prologue and twenty-one chapters drafted. The letter carries pieces as they are ready.

The Economics of Enough

It began on a hotel balcony in Istanbul, watching one of the best sunsets I had ever seen while looking at a spreadsheet. I had flown five thousand kilometres to stand somewhere beautiful and had managed to miss it entirely. The more uncomfortable thought arrived a moment later: I do this every day. I just do not usually have a sunset to measure it against.

This is the money half, written for India. Why the hedonic treadmill scales with income, so no salary ends the feeling on its own. What FIRE actually says once you scroll past the drone footage, and why the four percent rule breaks in India on four separate assumptions. How to find your own number, and why almost nobody knows the single most important input. And why enough is not one figure but a set of levels, each of which buys a different kind of freedom.

I went looking for someone to blame and found the arithmetic instead.

Draft complete, being revised. Some of it will change shape once the first book settles.
