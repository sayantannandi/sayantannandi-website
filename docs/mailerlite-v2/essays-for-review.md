# It felt like politics cost you the promotion

Preview: An unfair decision and an unclear case can exist in the same room.

Essay words: 1017

The promotion goes to someone whose work you know well. You cannot see what they did that you did not do.

You remember the evenings you worked. You remember the assignment that nearly fell apart until you took it over. Then you hear that you need to demonstrate more readiness, and the word feels carefully chosen to explain almost nothing.

Politics is a believable explanation.

I understand why that explanation has such a strong pull. It gives a name to the part of the decision you could not see. It also protects something important: the knowledge that you worked hard and that your work was useful. Being passed over does not erase either of those facts.

There are workplaces where favouritism decides who gets opportunities. There are managers who avoid honest feedback. A promotion can also disappear because the budget changed after somebody encouraged you to apply. A useful conversation about career growth has to leave room for those possibilities. Otherwise, it ends up telling every disappointed person that the organisation must have been right.

But there is another possibility worth examining alongside them. Your contribution may have been real while the evidence for the next role remained difficult to assess.

That distinction can be uncomfortable because it sounds like being asked to do the work twice. First you complete the assignment. Then you explain why it should count. Yet the people deciding on a promotion are usually trying to make a prediction. They are considering whether someone can handle responsibility they do not fully hold today.

A completed project gives them some evidence. It does not answer every part of that prediction.

Consider an illustrative example. Meera manages a reporting project. She delivers it on time, despite several changes to the requirements. In her review, she explains that she worked across departments and personally corrected the final report over a weekend. Every part of that account is true within this example.

The director reviewing candidates is considering a role that will own several reporting programmes at once. She hears that Meera can rescue one difficult project. She still cannot tell whether Meera can establish a process that prevents every programme from needing a personal rescue.

The mismatch is between the example being offered and the responsibility being considered. More detail about the weekend will not necessarily close it.

Before the conversation, Meera's case sounds like this: “I delivered the reporting project despite repeated changes. I stayed closely involved and made sure the final version was correct.”

After examining her contribution, she has a more specific account: “I introduced an approval point before changes entered the final report. That let the department heads resolve conflicting requests before the analysts rebuilt their work. The next reporting cycle used the same approval point without needing me to coordinate every change.”

The second version still needs to be true. If the next cycle has not happened, that final sentence does not belong in it. Clear language cannot substitute for experience that has not occurred.

What changes is the responsibility being described. Meera is explaining a decision about how work happens, along with evidence that the decision can support work beyond her own immediate effort.

There is a habit underneath this. Experienced professionals can become extremely good at collecting proof of dedication. They remember how much they absorbed and how quickly they responded. That record matters, but it can crowd out another record: the decisions they owned and the work other people became able to do.

I find that second record more useful when discussing readiness because it allows a more precise disagreement. A manager can say that the approval process worked but did not yet cover a large enough scope. That is a frustrating answer, perhaps, but it gives both people something concrete to examine.

“Be more strategic” offers much less.

There is also a timing problem. If the first discussion of the next role happens during the final review meeting, there may be no time left to build missing evidence. The promotion process could already be well advanced. A clear case prepared late can still arrive after the people involved have formed their view.

A readiness conversation earlier in the cycle has a different purpose. It establishes what responsibility the role includes, then examines which examples already demonstrate it. It also makes any disagreement about the standard visible while there is still time to observe more work.

This does not require pretending that a manager can promise a promotion. They may have no authority to guarantee a vacancy or a budget. What they can potentially explain is the work the role requires and the evidence they would find convincing.

The difference matters for earning power. Higher pay in a leadership role is usually connected to a larger area of responsibility. A case built entirely around doing more of the current job may leave the proposed increase in responsibility unexplained.

There is a boundary here too. If the criteria keep changing after you meet them, that is information about the organisation. If your manager cannot identify any assignment that would demonstrate readiness, the problem may extend beyond the wording of your case. A clearer record helps you see those situations more accurately. It should not become a reason to accept indefinite promises.

One useful way to examine a recent review is to separate the statements about your work from the statements about the next role. On one page, the completed assignments remain exactly as they happened. On another, the responsibilities of the proposed role are written in ordinary language. The gaps between those pages become visible without requiring a verdict on your worth as a person.

Some gaps will be about missing experience. Others will be about experience that was never explained clearly. They require different responses.

In Meera's example, the promotion conversation changes when the director can examine the approval process she introduced. The conversation may still end without a promotion. But it now concerns an identifiable responsibility and the evidence behind it.

The weekend rescue is still part of the record. It is no longer the whole case.

# AI can do part of your job. That changes the job.

Preview: The useful question sits inside the work, beyond the fear of the tool.

Essay words: 1028

An AI tool produces a first draft in less time than you would have spent finding the right file.

The draft looks competent. It uses the language your team uses. It even includes a conclusion that sounds reasonable.

That can be unsettling when producing those drafts has been a large part of your professional value.

I take that reaction seriously. It is difficult to feel curious about a tool while also wondering whether someone will use it to reduce the number of people needed for your work. A demonstration of faster output can look very different to the person planning next year's budget and the person whose salary is in that budget. There are two tempting responses. One is to dismiss the output because it makes mistakes. The other is to use the tool for everything and assume that familiarity will make a role secure.

Neither response explains which parts of the work still require a person to take responsibility.

A job contains several kinds of work. Some tasks produce a draft or organise information. Other tasks involve deciding what information belongs in the draft and whether the conclusion is supported. Those activities can sit so closely together that they feel like one skill until a tool separates them.

Consider an illustrative example involving Arjun, a programme manager. Every Friday, he prepares an update from the notes supplied by four project teams. Much of his time goes into making the language consistent and removing repeated information.

An approved AI tool can help with that work. It may produce a usable starting draft quickly. But the source notes contain a more difficult problem: one team describes a dependency as resolved, while another still lists it as a reason its deadline could slip.

A polished summary could smooth over the contradiction. The words would improve while the usefulness of the report got worse.

Arjun's contribution becomes more valuable when he notices that contradiction and identifies the decision it requires. Somebody has to establish whether the dependency is actually closed before the director can rely on the release date. That responsibility does not disappear because the first draft arrived faster.

Before, his update might say: “The programme is progressing, and the teams are coordinating to manage the remaining dependencies.”

After checking the source notes, it could say: “The release date depends on the access change being complete. The platform team reports it as closed, but the testing team still cannot use the new permissions. I recommend keeping the date provisional until both teams confirm the same working access.”

That is a different kind of contribution from producing a tidy paragraph.

It also shows why learning to use AI has to include learning to evaluate what comes back. If you cannot recognise the contradiction in the original material, a fluent summary may make it even harder to see. The quality of the output depends partly on the assignment and partly on the judgment applied afterwards.

The assignment itself can be more precise. Arjun might ask the approved tool to summarise each team's stated position separately and preserve any disagreement about dates. He can require the draft to point back to the supplied note for each claim. These instructions do not guarantee accuracy. They give him something more concrete to inspect.

There is a practical boundary around the information too. A workplace may permit one tool for internal documents and prohibit another. Customer information can have different restrictions from a public product description. Choosing an appropriate tool and permitted input is part of assigning the task, before the first prompt is written.

I am less interested in collecting clever prompts than in understanding where responsibility remains after a task is accelerated.

For Arjun, the saved drafting time creates a choice. He could fill it with more reports of the same kind. He could also use part of it to resolve the contradictions that previously survived inside those reports. The second option may provide stronger evidence for a role that owns programme decisions rather than merely consolidating programme updates.

That does not mean the organisation will automatically reward the change. It means the work now contains evidence of a different skill.

This is where earning power enters the conversation. If your value is described entirely as the time it takes to produce a document, faster production can put pressure on that description. If your contribution includes deciding whether the document supports a sound decision, the description becomes more specific about the responsibility you carry.

The distinction still needs evidence. Calling yourself the person responsible for judgment is easy. Showing how you caught an unsupported claim or changed a recommendation because the evidence was weak is more convincing.

One way to examine an AI-assisted task is to keep the first output beside the accepted version. The useful record is the difference between them and why each change was made. A corrected factual claim tells one story. A change to the recommendation because two sources conflict tells another.

Over several assignments, that record can show where the tool helps and where your own understanding needs work. It may reveal that you are accepting fluent explanations too quickly. It may also reveal that you are spending time rewriting sentences while leaving the underlying decision untouched.

Both are easier to notice with an actual draft than with a general promise to use AI responsibly.

The fear of being replaced can make every new capability feel like a verdict on an entire career. In practice, examining one recurring task gives a more useful view. Some parts may become faster. Other parts may become more demanding because more output is now available to assess.

There will still be decisions about staffing that an individual cannot control. No course or prompt library can guarantee protection from a redundancy decision. Building skill is a way to develop the work you can demonstrate, rather than a promise about what an employer will do.

In Arjun's example, the final report is shorter than the original summary. It contains a clear unresolved dependency and a recommendation about the date.

The tool drafted the words. Arjun's record shows why the final recommendation could be relied on.

# Everyone applying for the role looks qualified

Preview: A strong CV can leave the most important part of the choice unresolved.

Essay words: 1015

The shortlist contains people who have done much of what you have done.

They have managed projects of a similar size. Their qualifications look familiar. Several have spent roughly the same number of years at work.

Being good enough to apply no longer seems to make you different.

It is easy to respond by adding another credential. A qualification gives you something concrete to work towards, especially when the selection process feels difficult to read. There are roles where a particular qualification is essential. But once every candidate meets the basic requirements, adding another line to the same category may leave the choice largely unchanged. I think the more interesting distinction is what each candidate can help the interviewer understand.

An interviewer considering a larger role has a problem of their own. They need to choose someone who can handle work they cannot supervise constantly. Similar CVs tell them that several people deserve consideration. The conversation then has to provide evidence about how each person approaches responsibility.

This is why broad claims tend to lose their usefulness in a competitive shortlist. “I am results oriented” gives the interviewer little to examine. The other candidates can make the same claim, and there is no obvious way to compare them.

A specific account of a decision creates a different conversation.

Consider an illustrative example. Kavya and Rohan have both managed customer onboarding programmes. Each has worked with account teams and delivered process improvements. If their descriptions stop there, an interviewer may struggle to see a meaningful distinction.

Kavya describes a dashboard she introduced. Rohan describes how he identified a recurring delay between the signed contract and the first setup call. Neither description is automatically stronger. The quality depends on what they can show about the problem and the change.

Suppose Kavya's account continues like this: “Account managers previously checked four spreadsheets to find customers waiting for setup. The dashboard brought those records together, so each account manager could identify overdue setup calls from one view.”

Now the interviewer can see who used the work and what became possible because it existed.

Before, Kavya might have said: “I led a dashboard initiative and coordinated with several teams to improve onboarding.”

After, she can explain: “I noticed that account managers could not reliably identify customers who had signed but had no setup call booked. I brought those records into one view and agreed who would check the overdue list each morning. The useful change was that an unbooked customer became visible to a named person.”

This account does not need a dramatic number to be informative. If there is reliable evidence that waiting time fell, the number belongs in the explanation. If that evidence is not available, inventing precision would weaken the case.

The detail also makes questions easier to answer. The interviewer can ask how Kavya dealt with conflicting records or why the daily check belonged to account managers. Her reasoning becomes visible because the example has enough substance to examine.

That is a different form of distinction from trying to sound more impressive than the other candidates.

There is a familiar habit that can get in the way. After years of doing complex work, you may explain it using the internal project name and assume the importance is obvious. The name carries a history for you. To someone outside the project, it may carry almost nothing.

Translating the work means rebuilding the necessary context without retelling every meeting. The listener needs to understand the original difficulty and the consequence of leaving it unresolved. They then need to see what you did about it.

This is especially useful when the person evaluating you does not share your speciality. A finance director may not know why a particular technical change was difficult. They can still understand the cost of repeated manual checking or a decision that remained blocked until the change was made.

Clear translation does not reduce the technical work. It gives another person a way to understand why it deserved attention.

There is also a limit to the idea of standing out. An application can be distinctive and still be wrong for the particular role. A team hiring someone to stabilise an unreliable operation may prefer different evidence from a team preparing to enter a new market.

The purpose of the example is therefore more specific than making you memorable. It helps the interviewer assess your experience against the work they actually need done.

This changes preparation. Instead of collecting the most impressive-sounding achievements, you can examine which examples show the responsibility the role includes. A smaller assignment may demonstrate a relevant decision more clearly than a large project where your contribution is difficult to separate from everyone else's.

The same distinction applies inside an organisation. When several people deserve consideration for promotion, a manager has to explain why a particular person is ready for a particular responsibility. A clear example gives that explanation something firm to rest on.

Earning power grows through several routes, but the route this course focuses on requires being trusted with broader responsibility. Credentials can establish eligibility. Evidence of handling the work helps make the case for that trust.

One practical test of an example is whether a person unfamiliar with the project can describe its contribution after hearing it once. They do not need to repeat every detail. They should be able to explain what changed and why that change mattered to the people doing the work.

If they can only remember that the project was large and difficult, the explanation may still be centred on effort rather than contribution.

That does not mean the effort was unimportant. It means the listener has not yet received enough information to evaluate the result.

In Kavya's example, the dashboard is still part of her CV. The interview now contains something the CV could not fully show: how she recognised a problem in the handover between two stages of customer onboarding and made ownership of that problem explicit.

The candidates remain similarly qualified. The evidence available for comparing their work is no longer identical.

# The colleague who works less and gets promoted faster

Preview: Hours are visible. Some useful decisions are much harder to see.

Essay words: 1062

Your colleague leaves on time. You are still working.

A few months later, the promotion announcement includes their name. You think about the evenings when you were answering questions while they appeared to have finished for the day.

The comparison is difficult to dismiss.

I understand why hours become the obvious measure in that moment. You can see your own effort closely. You know what you gave up to finish the work. What you may not see as clearly is the work your colleague decided to stop or the decisions someone else became able to make without them.

That does not establish that the promotion was fair. Sometimes the person leaving on time is benefiting from work someone else is quietly carrying. Sometimes a manager rewards confidence while overlooking contribution. Those possibilities deserve examination rather than a convenient story about working smarter.

But a long working day can also contain work that should no longer depend on one person.

Consider an illustrative example involving two managers responsible for similar teams. Dev checks every customer proposal before it leaves his team. He knows the details well and catches mistakes. People trust him because he responds quickly, including in the evening.

Nisha also checks proposals, but she has separated routine cases from exceptions. Her team has an agreed standard for routine pricing. A proposal comes to her when it falls outside those limits or includes an unusual commitment.

Dev's calendar shows more activity. Nisha's team may be making more decisions without waiting.

The difference is not visible from their finishing times alone. It becomes visible when you examine where decisions go. Dev remains the final checkpoint for almost everything. Nisha has defined which decisions belong to the people preparing the proposal and which require her attention.

That definition is work. It takes time to agree a useful boundary and to review mistakes without taking every decision back. Once the agreement starts working, some of the effort becomes less visible because fewer requests arrive.

Before, Dev's handover sounds like this: “Prepare the proposal and send it to me before sharing it.”

After a more precise agreement, it might sound like this: “You can approve the standard package within the agreed pricing range. Bring it back if the customer requests a service commitment we have not made before. I will review a sample of routine proposals on Friday.”

There is still oversight. What changes is the dependence on immediate approval.

This matters for promotion because a larger role usually cannot be performed by applying the same level of personal attention to a larger number of tasks. If every decision must pass through one person, increasing the size of the role can make the existing bottleneck worse.

A person who establishes useful decision boundaries is demonstrating a way for the work to continue as responsibility expands.

There is another part of the comparison: the work that never enters the calendar. A manager may have declined a recurring report because nobody could identify a decision it supported. Another may continue producing it because stopping feels risky.

The second manager's extra hours are real. The report may still be an unnecessary use of those hours.

Saying no to that work responsibly requires more than deleting a meeting or ignoring a request. The cost of stopping has to be examined. Somebody may depend on the report in a way that is not obvious. A temporary pause with a review date can reveal whether the work is still needed without pretending there is no risk.

This is why I prefer a written reason for stopping work to a general ambition to become more efficient. The reason can be challenged. “I am too busy” describes your situation. “This report repeats information already used in the Monday review, and no separate decision has been identified” describes the work.

The connection to earning power is uncomfortable because it challenges an early career bargain. Early on, doing more yourself can be an effective way to become useful. Later, a higher-paid role may require making choices about what gets done and who can decide it.

The old habit can persist because it has earned praise. Being available feels safer than allowing someone else to make a decision you would make differently. Personally rescuing a deadline produces immediate gratitude. Preventing repeated rescue is less dramatic.

None of this makes long hours proof of poor judgment. A team can be understaffed. A crisis can require concentrated effort. Some roles have seasonal peaks that are difficult to avoid. The useful distinction is between a demanding period and a recurring system that depends on one person's permanent availability.

A closer examination of a recent week can reveal that distinction. The requests that arrived after hours can be grouped by the decision they required. Some will have been genuine exceptions. Others may have returned only because nobody had been told they could decide.

Those repeated approvals provide a more concrete starting point than comparing calendars with a colleague.

There is a risk in delegation too. If the agreement is vague, the person receiving the task may carry responsibility without enough authority. If every mistake leads to the work being taken back, they may learn to seek approval for everything again.

Useful delegation needs a clear result and a realistic boundary around decisions. It also needs a review arrangement that allows learning without making every action dependent on immediate supervision.

In the example, Nisha's shorter day does not prove she deserves promotion. The stronger evidence is that her team can handle routine proposals within an agreed standard and recognise when an exception needs attention.

A fair comparison would also examine who carries the consequences of the arrangement. If Nisha's shorter day is possible only because a junior colleague stays late every night, the responsibility has been displaced rather than handled well. A working delegation agreement should make the workload and the decision authority visible to the person receiving it. The evidence is therefore wider than whether the manager has stopped receiving messages. It includes whether the team can meet the agreed standard within a workable arrangement.

Dev may be capable of building the same arrangement. His current record shows how reliably he answers the questions. Nisha's shows which questions her team no longer needs to ask her.

At six in the evening, that difference can look like somebody simply leaving work earlier.

# Being indispensable can make a promotion complicated

Preview: The team depends on you. That can become part of the problem.

Essay words: 1060

You hear that the team would struggle without you.

It sounds like recognition. Then you ask about a larger role and discover that the same dependence has become a reason to keep you where you are.

The compliment has acquired a condition.

I find this one particularly uncomfortable because the behaviour behind it may have been encouraged for years. You learned the difficult process. You became the person who could explain the exceptions. When a colleague left, you absorbed the knowledge they had not documented. The organisation benefited from your reliability, and now a change in your role looks risky because so much rests on it.

That risk is real. It does not make an indefinite delay fair.

There are two separate decisions here. One concerns whether you are ready for broader responsibility. The other concerns how your current work would continue if your role changed. A manager may blur them together and leave you with a vague instruction to make yourself less essential.

That can sound like being asked to weaken your own position before anything is offered in return.

A better conversation makes the continuity problem specific. If the concern is that only you can approve an unusual customer exception, then that is the responsibility that needs a handover. If the concern is that several decisions exist only in your memory, a document alone may be insufficient. Someone else will need an opportunity to make those decisions and receive feedback.

Consider an illustrative example involving Farah, who manages an internal service. Whenever a request falls outside the usual process, it comes to her. She knows which exceptions have been approved before and which commitments the team cannot safely make.

Farah wants a role covering several services. Her manager is worried that the existing service will become unreliable if she moves. Both the ambition and the concern make sense.

Before, Farah's response might be: “I have documented everything, so the team should be able to manage.”

The document may describe the normal process well. The unresolved concern is what happens when the normal process does not fit.

After examining the actual requests, Farah can propose a more concrete arrangement: “Amit will decide routine exceptions within the existing service limit. Requests that add a new commitment will come to the service owner. For the next four weeks, we will review the exceptions together on Thursday, with Amit making the initial recommendation.”

This gives the manager a way to observe whether responsibility is moving.

The distinction between information and authority is important. A handover can transfer a large amount of information while leaving all the decisions with the original person. In that situation, the receiving colleague becomes better informed but still dependent.

The opposite problem is also possible. Someone may be told that they now own a process without receiving the authority to make the decisions it requires. They end up carrying the blame for a result while still waiting for permission to act.

A useful agreement connects the required result to the decisions the new owner can actually make. It also identifies the circumstances in which an exception needs to return for review.

This is a leadership skill because the work has to continue beyond your immediate attention. A larger role will bring responsibilities that cannot all be performed personally. The ability to establish ownership is part of the evidence that a larger role can function.

It is also a change in habit. Being needed can feel reassuring, especially when your professional identity rests on being the person who knows. Repeated requests provide visible confirmation of your value. A successful handover removes some of that confirmation.

The empty request queue can feel strangely like becoming less useful, even when it is evidence that something useful has been built.

That emotional part deserves a place in the discussion. It is difficult to delegate well while quietly hoping that every hard question will still come back. You may write an agreement that gives someone authority, then respond so quickly to every uncertainty that they never get to use it.

The record that matters is therefore what happens after the handover. A colleague making a sound recommendation without your intervention provides evidence of a changed arrangement. A document marked complete provides evidence that the document exists.

The connection to earning power comes through the scale of responsibility you can credibly carry. If a role depends entirely on your personal availability, its capacity is closely tied to your hours. When other people can own defined decisions, your contribution can include the quality of the wider arrangement.

That does not remove the need for an honest promotion conversation. A successful handover should not become an endless series of additional conditions. The scope and the review date belong in the discussion, along with what the manager is actually able to decide.

It is possible to complete a good handover and discover that there was never a role available. That is disappointing information, but it is different from continuing to assume that one more month of exceptional reliability will create an opening.

There is another useful test of the arrangement. Farah can be present during the review period without answering every question first. Amit's initial recommendation reveals what he understands and where the decision boundary is still unclear. Immediate rescue would hide that information from both of them.

In this example, the four-week review is not a test of whether Amit can become Farah. It is a test of whether the service has a workable owner for the decisions being transferred. The result might lead to a clearer boundary or a different allocation of responsibility.

The review can also reveal whether the receiving colleague has enough time for the new responsibility. A handover that adds ownership to an already full role may fail for reasons unrelated to capability. Farah and Amit need to discuss what existing work changes when he takes on the exceptions. Otherwise, the organisation may have transferred the dependence from one overextended person to another while calling the handover complete.

By the final review, the manager has a record of actual decisions made under the new arrangement. The conversation about Farah's next role can proceed with less uncertainty about the existing service.

Farah remains knowledgeable. The difference is that the service no longer requires her to be the first person asked about every exception.

# Good work can stay quiet for a very long time

Preview: Explaining a contribution can feel uncomfortably close to showing off.

Essay words: 1058

You would rather do the work than talk about it.

The people who speak most confidently about their achievements can seem exhausting. You do not want every meeting to become a performance, and you certainly do not want to take credit for something several people made possible.

So you keep the update brief and assume the result will be noticed.

I understand the instinct. There is a kind of self-promotion that makes ordinary contribution sound heroic. It can also erase the people who helped. Rejecting that style is reasonable. The difficulty begins when the rejection extends to any explanation of what your work changed.

A person outside your immediate team may see the output without understanding the contribution. They may know a system went live but have no idea which decision prevented it from creating more work for the support team.

Silence leaves that interpretation to someone else.

Consider an illustrative example involving Sanjay, who improves an internal request process. Before the change, requests often arrive without enough information. His team sends them back, and the requester waits for another review.

Sanjay changes the form and agrees which information is required before a request enters the queue. It is useful work. In the monthly update, he writes: “Completed the request-form changes.”

The sentence is accurate. It also makes the assignment sound like a minor editing task.

Before, his explanation identifies the thing produced. After, it can identify the difficulty addressed: “Requests were entering the queue without the information needed for review. I worked with the review team to define the required fields, so incomplete requests can now be identified before they take a place in the queue.”

That account does not claim that Sanjay transformed the organisation. It explains a specific contribution and credits the people involved.

If he has measured a reduction in returned requests, he can include that evidence. If the process has only just changed, the measured result remains open. The purpose of clear communication is to make the work easier to assess, including the parts that are not yet established.

This is where the fear of showing off can become misleading. An accurate explanation gives the listener information they need. An inflated explanation asks the listener to admire a conclusion that the evidence may not support.

Those are different communication choices.

There is also a practical reason to make the explanation portable. Your manager may need to discuss your work in a meeting where you are absent. They may have a few minutes to explain why a particular assignment demonstrates readiness for more responsibility.

A long project history is difficult to repeat. A clear account of the change is more likely to survive the conversation without becoming either vague or exaggerated.

I think of this as taking some responsibility for the accuracy of the account that travels beyond you. The work will be described somehow. Giving people a concise, supportable explanation reduces the amount they have to infer.

That explanation can still include uncertainty. “The new process is in place; the first month of data will show whether returned requests fall” is a stronger statement than implying a measured improvement before any measurement exists.

It can also include shared credit. “I defined the intake rule with the review team; Priya built the form” allows a listener to distinguish contributions without pretending that either person worked alone.

This becomes more relevant as your role grows beyond a speciality. People evaluating your contribution may not know the technical difficulty of the work. They need a route into the explanation that connects the specialist task to an understandable consequence.

The habit that needs to change is the assumption that familiarity is shared. Inside a project, a short name can summon months of context. Outside it, the same name is just a label.

A useful explanation therefore begins a little earlier than the output. It establishes the problem that made the work worth doing. It then names the change, without requiring the listener to reconstruct the entire assignment.

There is a connection to earning power here, but it is more precise than becoming louder. A case for a higher-paid leadership role needs evidence about the responsibility you have handled. If that evidence remains hidden inside shorthand, the case may be weaker than the work itself.

Speaking more often without improving the explanation will not necessarily help. A repeated vague claim is still vague.

One way to examine the difference is to read the last update as if you had never heard of the project. Any phrase that requires inside knowledge becomes visible. A project code might need replacing with the problem being solved. “Process improvement” might need replacing with what somebody can now do differently.

The rewritten version does not need to be longer. Sanjay's original update could expand into two useful sentences while a page of project history could shrink into the same two sentences.

The standard is whether the explanation carries enough meaning to be understood accurately.

There are limits to what communication can repair. If an organisation consistently takes your work for granted, a better update may not change its decisions. If your manager claims your contribution as their own, the problem is more serious than wording. A clear written record can still make the pattern easier to identify.

In an ordinary situation, though, the gap can be less dramatic. Nobody has decided to ignore the work. They simply have not received an explanation that makes its significance clear outside the people who performed it.

That is a gap a professional can learn to close without adopting a theatrical style.

A useful account also separates intention from result. Sanjay may have designed the form to reduce repeated reviews, but that result needs observation. Saying what the change is intended to do gives the next update a clear point of comparison. If requests continue to return, the account can explain what the first version missed. That is still evidence of taking responsibility for the work, even when the initial change does not perform as expected.

The gap may lead to an assignment in her current role or a more precise conversation about the support the new role would include. It does not need to become a judgment that she is either completely ready or entirely unqualified.

Readiness is more useful when it is described in terms of the actual work.

A clear conversation also makes an unsuitable role easier to recognise. If the promised authority is too limited for the accountability, the mismatch belongs in the decision. A larger salary may still make the role attractive, but the cost is more visible.

There is value in writing down the expected pattern of attention as well. Ritu can ask which decisions require an immediate response and which belong in a scheduled review. If every exception is treated as urgent, the shared review may exist on paper while the old interruptions continue. The agreement needs to describe ordinary working practice, including what happens when one of the team managers is unavailable. Those details affect the lived shape of the role.

In the example, Ritu and the hiring manager agree which decisions belong to the team managers and which cross-team decisions come to her. The promotion remains a demanding move. It now has an operating arrangement that can be discussed and reviewed.

The title is the same as the one she originally wanted. The responsibility behind it has become much clearer.

## Course practice: The Readiness Conversation

The Narrative Card and Readiness Note bring relevant evidence into a conversation about a proposed role. The practice connects completed work to the responsibility being considered and identifies what remains unproven. It also makes the requested scope more precise than an offer to take on additional tasks.
